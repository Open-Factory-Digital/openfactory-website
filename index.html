Software stopped being handmade.

A ticket today is craft work: someone opens an editor, hunts through the codebase, writes the change, runs the tests, opens the PR. OpenFactory puts that on a line. A card goes in, and a reviewed pull request comes out, built in a sandbox running your project's own gates and checked by a second, independent agent before it ever reaches you.

Your day moves with it. Instead of reading every diff as it is typed, you read the floor: what shipped, what stalled, what needs a decision only a person can make. The two calls that were always yours, what gets built and whether it ships, stay yours.

The unit of capacity

A faster developer is still one developer.

Codex, Claude Code, Kimi and the rest raise the output of one person at a keyboard, and they are sold the way they work: one licence, one person, one conversation at a time. The unit of capacity is still a human, because somebody has to start each task, stay with it, and carry it to a pull request.

OpenFactory does not compete with those tools. It runs them. The agents you already pay for stop being a personal assistant and become a line the company owns, and the number you manage stops being cost per developer per month and becomes cost per merged ticket. The people do not disappear from that picture. They stop being the conveyor belt between a ticket and a pull request, and keep the decisions that need judgment.

The same agents appear on both sides. What changes is where the person sits, and whether the capacity belongs to a licence or to the company.

Provider-neutral by design

Connects to what you already run

OpenFactory sits between the board where a ticket already lives and the coding agent that works it. It drives the agents you already use instead of adding another one, and each connection is a registry value, not a fork: swap the tracker without touching the harness, or swap the harness without touching anything else.

on any impediment

For the people who will run it

It takes the boring half.

You keep the judgment

Policy authorizes the routine path, so nobody spends a morning approving test runs. What gets built, whether it ships, and every promotion to production stay human calls. The mechanical middle of a ticket was never the interesting part.

It never lies to you

An unreadable board is not an empty queue, and a failed read is never dressed up as a result. Every stall either self-heals within a bound or asks you a concrete question, and going quiet is treated as a bug by the test suite.

Your machines, your gates, your fork

docker compose up and it runs, with no cloud account. It executes your project's own commands instead of naming a build tool of its own, and it is Apache-2.0: read it, run it, fork it, add a provider with one module and one registry row.
